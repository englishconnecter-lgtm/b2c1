B2 Grammar · Reported Speech

Use reported speech to summarise what someone said, asked or told another person to do.

  • Statements. Use said (that) or told + person + (that). With a past reporting verb, tenses often shift back: is → was, have seen → had seen, will → would. Backshift can be optional when the information still applies.
  • Questions. Use asked if/whether for yes/no questions. Keep the question word in wh-questions and use statement word order: She asked what he knew.
  • Advice, instructions and suggestions. Use advised/told + person + (not) to + verb or suggested + -ing.
  • Pronouns and time expressions. Update them when the context changes: “I’ll publish my findings tomorrow” → She said she would publish her findings the following day.

Report what was said

Choose the best reported version. Some examples are adapted for practice.

1. “A small change will have a disastrous impact.”
The speaker claimed that a small change ___ a disastrous impact.

2. “My sister says her students are too immature.”
The speaker said that his sister ___ her students were too immature.

3. “Have you seen the size of his pick-up truck?”
The speaker asked the audience whether ___ the size of his pick-up truck.

4. “What does he know about protecting the environment?”
The speaker asked what he ___ about protecting the environment.

5. “Pay attention to what politicians are saying.”
The presenter advised viewers ___ attention to what politicians were saying.

6. “Don’t ignore the evidence.”
The moderator told the audience ___ the evidence.

7. “We should spend less money on the military.”
The debater suggested ___ less money on the military.

8. “I’ll publish my findings tomorrow,” Maya told us.
A week later, we reported that Maya had told us ___ findings the following day.

Thought-Provoking Questions

Discuss these questions in pairs or small groups. Give reasons, examples and possible consequences.

1. Which logical fallacy do you see most often in politics or social media?

2. Can emotional appeals be ethical, or are they always manipulative?

3. Why are simple stories so powerful in debates about complex issues?

4. Should schools teach students how to identify faulty arguments?

5. Is it worse to use a personal attack or a strawman argument?

6. How can voters check whether a politician is presenting facts fairly?

7. Can a speaker use a fallacy accidentally, or is it usually deliberate?

Challenge: Choose one question and prepare a one-minute answer using cautious language such as: It could be argued that..., One possible concern is..., This becomes more complicated because...
