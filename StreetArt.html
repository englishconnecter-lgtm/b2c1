Why street art doesn’t always make a city better

B2 reading-comprehension lesson based on a shortened version of an article from The Conversation. Practise reading for main ideas, detail, vocabulary, grammar and critical thinking.

Before you read

Street art can make a city more vibrant, but it can also create problems if it is badly planned or disconnected from the community. This lesson asks when urban art improves quality of life and when it becomes only decoration.

Reading Comprehension

Read the original article on The Conversation

Read the shortened text. Then complete the comprehension exercise.

For many years, street art has often been understood in a limited way. Some people see it as decoration, a tourist attraction or a way to make a city look better. Others see it as a visual change imposed on them. The article argues that neither view is complete.

The key question is not simply whether a mural is popular, or whether a sculpture looks good in photos. The more important question is how a public artwork changes the everyday experience of a town or city. In this sense, urban art is connected to quality of life, because it can influence how people experience public space.

Street art can introduce beauty, calm and space to think into everyday life. However, it should not be judged only by its visual impact. A work of urban art can help a city become more integrated, balanced and meaningful for the people who live there.

Well-known urban artists often create moments of surprise and emotion. The article mentions Banksy’s Girl with Balloon as an example of a simple image that can produce strong feelings. However, famous names also create an unspoken hierarchy: some artists are allowed to intervene in public spaces, while others are excluded.

According to the article, it is not enough simply to occupy a wall or produce any image. Public art requires skill, permission, discussion and an understanding of the local area, responsibility and the ability to create a meaningful experience.

The article then introduces the AUPART research project, which studies urban art and quality of life. This project does not assume that every work of urban art automatically improves collective wellbeing. Instead, it asks whether that assumption is true and under what conditions art can make a positive difference.

Quality of life is presented as a complex idea. It includes material conditions, social relations, how people see their surroundings, how safe they feel, how the city is run, personal wellbeing and everyday experience. For this reason, public art should be evaluated in relation to the social and physical context around it.

Some projects show how urban art can support communities. The article refers to Mural Arts Philadelphia and the Inside Out Project as examples with community impact. It also discusses Julia, a sculpture by Jaume Plensa in Madrid, which offers a human presence in the city and can help mediate between people and public space.

However, good artistic quality does not guarantee social success. Richard Serra’s Tilted Arc in New York was criticised because it interrupted the everyday use of Federal Plaza, forcing people to change their usual routes and raising concerns about accessibility and safety.

The article also warns against believing that street art automatically regenerates an area. In Wynwood, Miami, murals created visibility and a strong urban brand, but the area also became more commercial and began to look more like other areas. Mural Istanbul is described as another project that was criticised for treating activism mainly as aesthetics.

The conclusion is that street art cannot, by itself, dramatically improve life in a city. It can help when it strengthens bonds with place, encourages people to spend time together and builds a sense of belonging and helps people understand their surroundings more deeply. For positive results, projects must be prepared carefully, socially relevant, connected to the local context and assessed for their real impact on community life.

Grammar: the passive voice (B2)

When to use the passive

Use the passive to focus on the person or thing affected by an action. The person who did it can be left out when they are unknown, obvious or unimportant. Use by when it matters who did it.

From the reading: Richard Serra’s Tilted Arc in New York was criticised because it interrupted the everyday use of Federal Plaza.

Form: subject + the correct form of be + past participle. When you change an active sentence to the passive, keep the tense or modal verb.

  • Present simple: Murals are painted on this wall every year.
  • Past simple: The sculpture was designed by a local artist.
  • Present continuous: A new mural is being painted.
  • Present perfect: The plan has been approved.
  • Past perfect: The wall had been cleaned before work began.
  • Future and modals: The plan will be discussed. Local residents must be consulted.
